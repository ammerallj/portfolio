How to edit
-----------
Each topic below is one block. Change the ANSWER text freely; that's what
visitors read. It's written in the first person, in your voice.

  TOPIC      A short name for the topic.
  TAGS       Words people might use for it. More = easier to find.
  ASKED AS   Example questions, separated by | . Write them the way a
             stranger would type them.
  NAME IT    Optional. Words that, when someone names them outright (a
             project, "Maeve"), jump straight to this topic.
  LINK       Optional. Label | where it goes. One LINK line per link.
  OPEN       Optional. A pill to offer: maeve, fashion, or start.
  ANSWER     What the chat says. A blank line starts a new paragraph.

To add a topic, copy the NEW TOPIC template at the very bottom.
Only use things that are public, or that you're happy for anyone to read.
Anything here can be quoted back to a visitor.

TOPIC: Facebook Messaging
ID: messaging
TAGS: messaging, messenger, facebook, meta, conversation, conversations, private, sharing, post-send, thread, entry, points, continuity, 2023
ASKED AS: tell me about the messaging project | what did you do on facebook messaging | what is the messaging case study | how did you improve messaging | what did you ship for messenger | what did you do at meta for conversations | how did you increase thread opens
NAME IT: facebook messaging, messaging, messenger, thread opens
LINK: Messaging case study | work/messaging.html
OPEN: 
ANSWER:
Facebook had lots of places where someone could send a message, but each entry point had evolved on its own. Depending on where you started, the next step could feel completely different.

I looked at those moments as one experience and defined the routing model for what should happen after send—when to confirm an action, stay in a lightweight thread, or continue into a fuller conversation. The broader effort addressed more than 100 million app switches to Messenger, and the shipped post-send experience increased thread opens by about 22% on iOS and 27% on Android.

What I liked about this work was that the design challenge wasn't another composer. It was making the entire journey feel connected. The full case study is confidential.

LINK: Accessibility at Meta | https://www.meta.com/accessibility/
OPEN: 
ANSWER:
Accessibility touched so many teams at Meta, but people often had to figure out what applied to their product, where to find guidance, and what to do next. A document full of standards wasn't going to solve that on its own.

I worked on how accessibility decisions fit into product workflows: capturing issues where they happened, turning requirements into usable guidance, and defining how AI-assisted retrieval could bring up the right information for a product question. The broader review automation saved over 1,700 hours of manual work.

I also designed Meta's centralized public accessibility destination, bringing together more than 30 features and 45 support articles around people's needs. Different audiences, same underlying question: how do we make the next step clearer? The full case study is confidential, but the public site is live.

OPEN: 
ANSWER:
Loop started with an interesting question: what happens when a piece of content isn't confined to one document or app anymore? A component could live in Teams and other Microsoft 365 surfaces, stay in sync, and still need to feel like the same object.

I helped define the early interaction model and visual identity, especially the cues that made a component recognizable wherever it appeared. Those ideas shipped in Teams, influenced developer guidance, and contributed to a patent.

I loved that the problem was bigger than a screen. We were helping define the behavior of a new kind of collaborative object.

Facebook Groups had a lot happening at the top of Group Home, sometimes before people even reached the content they came for. But simply removing everything would have taken away useful context too.

ASKED AS: why do you care about systems | do you prefer systems or features | why design across products | why are shared patterns important | how do you handle design systems | do you work on design systems
NAME IT: 
OPEN: 
ANSWER:
When a product grows, little inconsistencies start adding up. One team solves a problem one way, another team solves it differently, and eventually the experience asks people to relearn the same thing over and over.

I like finding the behavior that should be shared, without flattening everything into one generic pattern. A good system should make the experience more coherent and give teams more room to do thoughtful work—not less.
